# Task 07: Results, Discussion and Presentation

## 7.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the recorded results of the music genre classification system developed in the preceding chapters. The analysis addresses two operational criteria established in Section 1.3: whether the engineered audio descriptors supported at least 70% test accuracy, and whether the optimized RBF support vector machine (SVM) achieved higher observed test accuracy than the strongest evaluated baseline. Macro precision, macro recall, and macro F1 provide complementary evidence. Class-level errors and training diagnostics are examined to establish the scope and limitations of these findings.[^ch7-context]

The evidence comprises the saved evaluation tables, confusion matrices, search summaries, and analysis outputs. Source code and configuration establish how these artifacts were produced; they are not treated as evidence that every implemented or proposed procedure was executed. The chapter reports the completed experiments without adding training runs or new evaluations. Calculations and visualizations derived from saved results are identified explicitly.

## 7.2 Overview of Evaluated Experiments

The experiments used the local GTZAN-derived feature table described in Chapters 3 and 4. Each recording was represented by 61 predictors covering tempo, zero-crossing rate, RMS energy, spectral descriptors, MFCC statistics, and chroma statistics. Removing 13 exact full-row duplicates from 1,000 observations left 987 observations. The stratified 80/20 split with seed 30 yielded 789 training and 198 test observations. Test support was 20 observations per genre except for `metal`, which had 18. These counts concern feature rows; duplicate removal did not establish unique artists or unique underlying recordings.[^ch7-data]

Five-fold shuffled stratified cross-validation (CV), also with seed 30, operated within the training partition. There was no additional fixed validation partition. Standardization and optional supervised feature selection were fitted inside each training pipeline. The globally standardized exploratory table and PCA visualizations from Task 04 were not classifier inputs. Hyperparameters were selected by mean CV accuracy. However, the scripts subsequently ranked model variants by test accuracy and then test macro F1. Consequently, the common holdout was excluded from fitting but was not independent of every model-selection decision. The reported differences must be interpreted within this qualification.[^ch7-method]

Table 7.1 identifies the evaluated procedures and their evidential roles. The separate forest reference and analysis SVM were refits under the shared protocol, rather than additional model families or independent replications. The consolidated result file reproduced the nine baseline and two target rows; it did not constitute another eleven-model experiment.

Accuracy is the fraction of correctly assigned labels. Class precision is the fraction of predictions of a genre that were correct; class recall is the fraction of that genre's observations recovered. Class F1 is their harmonic mean. Macro scores average the ten class-specific values with equal weights; macro F1 is not calculated from macro precision and macro recall. Undefined divisions were assigned zero. Unless stated otherwise, metrics are proportions rounded to four decimal places. Absolute accuracy differences expressed on a percentage scale use percentage points; relative changes are explicitly labelled. Timings are reported in seconds and counts as integers. Calculations use the unrounded source values. A displayed standard deviation (SD) summarizes the recorded folds or permutations, not a confidence interval; very small nonzero SDs can round to 0.0000.[^ch7-method]

The CSV artifacts take precedence over conflicting historical prose. In particular, the timing table in `docs/assignment8.md` reported 1.06 s for the forest reference and 8.10 s for the all-feature SVM, whereas the current comparison CSV records 0.7851 s and 5.0831 s. The same document presented pooled-looking search ranks of 3 and 5 where the exported searches assigned within-grid ranks of 2 and 3. The current artifacts and implementation establish the values and rank semantics used below, but do not establish why the historical text differs. Earlier descriptions of an entirely untouched final test set are also qualified by the implemented ranking procedure. These distinctions agree with Tasks 04–06.[^ch7-history][^ch7-comparison][^ch7-search]

The most-frequent Dummy classifier achieved 0.1010 test accuracy and 0.0183 macro F1. Its saved class report showed predictions only for `blues`, with recall 1.0000 for that genre and zero recall for the others. It therefore supplied an actual frequency-based baseline without using the audio descriptors. Every descriptor-dependent baseline exceeded it on all four test metrics; this demonstrated predictive information relative to that reference within the evaluated dataset, without establishing that the learned associations were free from dataset-specific effects.[^ch7-reports]

Logistic Regression achieved 0.6111 test accuracy and 0.6061 macro F1 in both variants. Tuning selected `C=1.0` with `lbfgs`, matching the default setting for the searched parameters. Its identical scores therefore do not represent two distinct successful parameter choices. Its mean CV accuracy, 0.6831, was the highest among the baseline rows, although the forest led on the holdout. This change in ordering illustrates why a single validation or test ranking should not be generalized to all possible partitions.

The Decision Tree achieved 0.4495 test accuracy by default and 0.4444 after tuning selected `max_depth=10` and `min_samples_leaf=1`. Tuning increased its mean CV accuracy from 0.4309 to 0.4385, but all four test metrics declined. The single-tree models provided substantially weaker results than the evaluated ensembles. This observation concerns these fitted configurations; the artifacts do not isolate which architectural property caused the difference.

Random Forest provided the strongest test baseline. The default 100-tree model achieved 0.6919 accuracy and 0.6892 macro F1, while the tuned configuration with 200 trees, unrestricted depth, and `max_features="sqrt"` achieved 0.6818 and 0.6795. Tuning improved all four mean CV metrics but reduced all four test metrics. The default 100-tree setting was outside the tuned grid, which searched 200 and 500 trees. Thus, the label *tuned* did not imply superiority over the separately evaluated default.

Gradient Boosting improved from 0.6313 to 0.6465 test accuracy and from 0.6325 to 0.6470 macro F1 after selection of 200 stages, learning rate 0.1, and depth 2. All four test metrics increased. Its mean CV accuracy, recall, and F1 also increased, although mean CV precision declined. The optimized boosting model remained below both forest variants on all four test metrics. Taken together, the baseline results supported the forest as the strongest recorded holdout reference while showing that tuning benefits were neither universal nor uniform across evaluation measures.[^ch7-baseline][^ch7-method]

## 7.4 Comparative Analysis

### 7.4.1 Aggregate Improvement on the Common Holdout

Table 7.7 compares the all-feature SVM with the default Random Forest, the strongest baseline by recorded test accuracy. Their inputs, cleaning rule, partition settings, and evaluated sample counts were shared. Differences were calculated by subtracting each forest score from the corresponding SVM score using the full stored precision.

**Table 7.7. Test-score differences between the primary SVM and strongest recorded baseline.**

| Test metric | Default Random Forest | All-feature SVM | SVM minus forest |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Accuracy | 0.6919 | 0.7121 | +0.0202 |
| Macro precision | 0.6919 | 0.7161 | +0.0242 |
| Macro recall | 0.6933 | 0.7122 | +0.0189 |
| Macro F1 | 0.6892 | 0.7121 | +0.0228 |

*Source: Own calculation from `assignment8_comparative_results.csv`, cross-checked against the original baseline and target tables. Recorded accuracy and F1 difference fields agree with these calculations.[^ch7-comparison][^ch7-baseline][^ch7-target]*

The SVM achieved higher values on all four aggregate test metrics. Its accuracy increase was 2.02 percentage points; its macro F1 increase was 0.0228. Relative to the forest scores, these represented increases of 2.92% in accuracy and 3.31% in macro F1, calculated as the score difference divided by the forest score. Relative percentages and absolute percentage-point differences describe different quantities.

The confusion matrices contained 137 correct predictions and 61 errors for the forest, compared with 141 correct predictions and 57 errors for the SVM. The difference was therefore four additional correct predictions in aggregate. The matrices do not identify which individual recordings changed from correct to incorrect or vice versa, so the result cannot be described as four particular errors corrected without other changes. No paired significance test or independent confirmation of the difference was available.

The SVM also had higher mean CV accuracy, 0.7275 versus 0.6692. Its recorded fold SD was 0.0130 versus 0.0309 for the reference forest. These values describe the particular folds; the SVM configuration had been selected using those folds, whereas the forest reference had one candidate. They do not establish general superiority or an uncertainty interval for the complete selection process. The two operational criteria were met as observed holdout results, with the modest magnitude and test-based ranking limitation retained.

For the SVM, `classical` had the highest F1, 0.9268, with 19 correct assignments out of 20. Both `jazz` and `pop` had recall 0.8500, with 17 correct assignments each. At the weaker end, `disco` had precision, recall, and F1 of 0.4500, while `rock` had recall 0.5000 and F1 0.5128. This concentration shows that aggregate accuracy did not imply uniform genre recognition.

The largest directed SVM confusion was `disco` predicted as `country`, with four observations. Three `hiphop` observations were assigned to `reggae`, and three `metal` observations were assigned to `disco`. Precision and recall also reveal different error profiles. For `disco`, the model both missed 11 true observations and assigned 11 other observations to that label. These are distinct weaknesses that would be obscured by reporting accuracy alone.

Relative to the forest, SVM F1 increased for `country`, `hiphop`, `pop`, `reggae`, and `rock`, but decreased for the other five genres. The largest increase was for `pop`, from 0.6818 to 0.8293; the largest decrease was for `disco`, from 0.5556 to 0.4500. Jazz recall stayed at 0.8500 while its F1 declined, illustrating the contribution of false positives. The overall improvement therefore involved class-specific tradeoffs rather than consistent gains for every genre.

The errors are compatible with imperfect separation in the global descriptor representation. Overlapping acoustic summaries are one possible explanation, but the matrices alone cannot establish instrumentation, rhythm, production style, or annotation ambiguity as the cause of any individual error. No persistent recording identifiers, saved prediction-level examples, or listening annotations were available to test those explanations. The discussion consequently concerns observed label relationships, not invented examples of misclassified audio.

## 7.5 Interpretation and Discussion

### 7.5.1 Training Fit, Validation Behaviour, and Data Availability

The search-selected SVM achieved mean training-fold accuracy of 0.9861 against mean validation accuracy of 0.7275, a gap of 0.2585. The separately evaluated default forest reference had mean training accuracy of 0.9990 and validation accuracy of 0.6692, a gap of 0.3299. Both fitted the training observations much more closely than their validation observations. The smaller SVM gap did not remove this discrepancy, and high training accuracy cannot substitute for its test result.[^ch7-comparison]

Shuffling `mfcc_9_mean` produced the largest mean decrease, 0.0448, corresponding to 4.48 percentage points. MFCC statistics accounted for most of the displayed features, alongside RMS statistics and `chroma_4_std`. This result is consistent with the fitted classifier depending on timbral, energy-related, and pitch-class summaries. The ranking does not measure the intrinsic importance of these properties to musical genre. Correlated descriptors can substitute for one another, while independently shuffling a feature can create combinations absent from the original recordings. Nine predictors had negative mean importance in the full file, meaning their shuffling slightly improved accuracy on average in this diagnostic; this does not establish that removing them and retraining would improve performance. The permutation SDs quantify shuffle variation, not uncertainty across independently trained models. Neither a plausible ranking nor a positive importance establishes that dataset-specific shortcuts were absent.

### 7.5.4 Relation to the Thesis Aims and Existing Literature

The recorded results supported the local engineering objective: the compact feature table enabled ten-class prediction above the project threshold, and the SVM achieved a modest improvement over the strongest holdout baseline. The implementation produced inspectable results for all planned classical model families, with common fitting boundaries and class-level diagnostics. Reproducibility was supported by configuration and input/source hashes, although complete run-to-output traceability remained limited as discussed in Section 7.7. The findings therefore establish the performance of the documented experimental pipeline, not a generally reliable audio-classification service.

The literature already reviewed in Chapter 2 provides qualitative context. Tzanetakis and Cook motivated the use of timbral, rhythmic, and pitch-related descriptors, while Bergstra and colleagues examined aggregated features. The present results are consistent with the usefulness of compact audio summaries for genre prediction, but neither study's feature construction was reproduced. Panagakis and colleagues also used an RBF SVM, with spectro-temporal representations and a different validation design; sharing a classifier family does not make the experiments equivalent.[^ch7-literature]

Choi and colleagues' comparison of transferred representations with MFCC-based inputs, as documented in Section 2.3, motivates investigating representation choice. No transferred encoder or spectrogram classifier was evaluated here, so the current score cannot be used to quantify an advantage or disadvantage against those methods. Similarly, the evaluation cautions discussed by Sturm and the confounding interventions reviewed from Rodríguez-Algarra and colleagues are relevant to the incomplete recording metadata and absence of artist-disjoint assessment in this project. They motivate caution; they do not establish that a specific confound caused the present errors.[^ch7-literature]

No reviewed study shared the exact local feature table, cleaning, partition, and model-selection procedure. Consequently, the literature comparison remains qualitative. A cross-paper ranking or numerical claim of improvement would combine different experimental conditions and is not justified by the available repository evidence. No additional literature or bibliography entries are introduced.

## 7.6 Strengths and Weaknesses

A principal strength was the coherent comparison of classical classifiers on the same representation and holdout. The actual Dummy reference demonstrated the contribution of descriptor-dependent prediction relative to a frequency rule, while the forest and SVM comparison addressed the stated research objective directly. Pipeline-based scaling and selection respected the fitting boundaries described in Task 04. The modular extraction, configuration, training, and analysis scripts described in Task 05 made the origin of each output inspectable. These are supported engineering characteristics, rather than evidence of deployed-service performance.

The saved CSVs supplied a stronger evidential basis than a single headline accuracy. Class reports, integer confusion matrices, validation summaries, and learning-curve points allowed aggregate scores to be checked and weaknesses to be localized. The latest manifest's configuration, canonical data, and five source hashes matched the corresponding repository files. This supported identification of the current experimental inputs, although it did not resolve all archival limitations.[^ch7-manifest]

The main predictive weakness was uneven genre performance. Disco and rock remained difficult for the selected SVM, and its aggregate gain coincided with lower F1 for half the genres. The substantial training–validation gaps were another weakness: very high fitted accuracy did not transfer to unseen fold observations. Global aggregation necessarily discarded temporal order, but no representation ablation established how much of the residual error it caused. The evidence supports further investigation of these constraints rather than attributing all errors to one design decision.

A further weakness was the separation between reproducible script structure and complete experimental independence. Ranking variants on the test partition and reusing tuning folds limited the strength of performance claims. The system was an experimental pipeline without a saved deployed predictor or an end-to-end audio-service benchmark. Independent final assessment, richer provenance, and comparison with temporal representations would be possible future improvements; they were not completed components of this work.

## 7.7 Limitations

### 7.7.1 Data Limitations

The cleaned dataset contained 987 feature observations from one local collection and ten supplied genre labels. The source excerpts were documented as approximately 30 seconds long, but individual durations, sampling rates, acquisition details, and artist identities were not catalogued in the feature table. The extractor retained the source sampling rate and summarized each loaded recording; it did not establish a fixed-duration segmentation experiment. These conditions limit the interpretation of results for longer tracks, differently sourced audio, or other genre taxonomies.[^ch7-data]

Exact full-row duplicate removal reduced direct repetition in the numerical table but did not verify audio identity, near-duplicates, or artist independence. The absence of recording and artist identifiers prevented a documented grouped evaluation. Potential overlap is therefore a justified concern, not an established finding that leakage occurred. Conversely, the cleaning procedure cannot support a claim that all such risks were eliminated. Genre labels originated from source folders, and no independent relabelling or listening-based quality assessment was recorded.

### 7.7.2 Methodological Limitations

All evaluated classifiers used a fixed family of global descriptors. There was no experiment with waveform input, spectrogram networks, transferred embeddings, temporal feature sequences, or feature-family ablation. PCA and globally scaled features belonged to exploration, and no performance effect of scaling was isolated experimentally. The selector search assessed a specific univariate filter and bounded feature counts; its selected all-feature outcome does not rule out other selection procedures.

The hyperparameter grids were finite and differed in size across model families. The search established the best recorded settings within those grids, not optimal performance for each classifier family. Training-fold validation was reused after tuning, and the fifteen-row sensitivity extract did not add independent repetitions. Learning-curve observations support conclusions only over their recorded sizes. No optimization-loss histories were saved; the accuracy-versus-data-size curve must not be interpreted as evidence about loss convergence or epoch-wise training behaviour.

### 7.7.3 Evaluation and Traceability Limitations

Only one common 198-observation holdout was available. One additional correct prediction would change overall accuracy by approximately 0.51 percentage points, derived as 100/198. Within a 20-observation genre, one changed prediction would alter recall by 5.00 percentage points; for the 18-observation metal class, the change would be 5.56 points. Class rankings and small aggregate differences must therefore be read in the context of limited support.

Test-based ranking limited independent final assessment, and selected-model CV scores were conditional on the same folds used for optimization. Recorded fold SDs did not supply confidence intervals or statistical significance for model differences. No repeated-split outcome archive, nested evaluation, artist-disjoint test, or external-dataset evaluation was available. The retained results therefore could not establish reliable generalization to unseen audio sources.

The class matrices supported aggregate error analysis, but the absence of persistent prediction-level records prevented paired recording-level comparisons and verified audio examples. Permutation importance reused the holdout and provided a descriptive diagnostic, not independent confirmation. No saved scores supported ROC or precision–recall curves, and no such curves were reconstructed from aggregate accuracy.

All ten manifest entries recorded a dirty working tree, and the manifest omitted result-file hashes. Matching the latest input and source hashes improved traceability but could not uniquely associate every current CSV with an execution. Multiple manifest records did not constitute independently archived repeated experiments. Raw audio was external, which further limited reconstruction of the audio-to-feature stage. These archival constraints explain why numerical claims are tied to named saved artifacts instead of unsupported assumptions about run histories.[^ch7-manifest]

## 7.8 Chapter Summary

The all-feature RBF SVM achieved 0.7121 test accuracy and 0.7121 macro F1, compared with 0.6919 and 0.6892 for the strongest recorded baseline, the default Random Forest. This met both operational criteria on the common holdout, with four additional correct predictions in aggregate. The selector variant retained all 61 features and supplied no additional score gain.

Class-level evidence qualified the aggregate result: classical was recognized strongly, whereas disco and rock contributed a substantial share of errors, and SVM F1 improved for only half the genres relative to the forest. Learning curves and search summaries showed strong training fit alongside lower validation performance. These findings support a bounded assessment of the implemented feature-based system while leaving broader reliability unresolved because of the single collection, limited holdout, and selection-related constraints. The project-wide conclusions remain the subject of Task 08.
