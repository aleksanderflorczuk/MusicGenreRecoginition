# 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Scope and Purpose of the Review

Music genre recognition is a supervised learning problem in which a system predicts a genre label from a recording. The central engineering challenge is to construct a representation that preserves useful musical information and to evaluate the resulting predictions under conditions that support the intended conclusions. The early work of Tzanetakis and Cook established an influential approach based on timbral, rhythmic, and pitch-related descriptors.[^tzanetakis2002] This thesis follows that general approach by representing each recording with a fixed-length vector of engineered audio features.

The review examines four connected themes: engineered features and classical classifiers; learned audio representations; datasets and genre annotations; and the reliability of experimental evaluation. Its purpose is to justify the comparison introduced in Chapter 1: whether compact audio descriptors support ten-class classification, and whether an RBF-kernel support vector machine offers an observable improvement over the selected baselines under a shared experimental protocol.

This is a targeted thematic review. It develops the bibliography collected for the project and supplements it with a related journal publication and a more recent study of self-supervised music representation learning. The selected publications span 2002–2024; their bibliographic records and relevant claims were rechecked on 14 October 2026. Older studies are retained because they introduced the benchmark, feature-based methods, or evaluation problems directly relevant to this project. The review does not claim an exhaustive search of all publications available in 2026.

Priority is given to original journal articles and conference papers. Official benchmark pages and task overviews provide evidence about evaluation procedures, while an explicitly identified preprint supplies additional documentation of GTZAN. These source types have different evidential roles: a task specification establishes a protocol, whereas an experimental paper can support a claim about observed performance. The comparison therefore considers credibility, relevance, publication date, and methodological limitations rather than treating every source as equally strong evidence.

## 2.2 Engineered Audio Features and Classical Classification

Tzanetakis and Cook combined descriptors of timbral texture, rhythm, and pitch, reporting 61% accuracy on a ten-genre collection associated with the subsequent GTZAN benchmark. Their experiments used statistical classifiers, including Gaussian models and nearest-neighbour methods. The contribution was the demonstration that audio content could support genre classification, rather than a particular modern classifier architecture.[^tzanetakis2002] The present thesis adopts the principle of feature-based classification, but its 61 predictors are a different representation and do not reproduce the original feature set.

Bergstra and colleagues investigated the aggregation of short-time descriptors and used AdaBoost with decision stumps or small decision trees. Their work illustrates that the way frame-level information is summarized can be as important as the final classifier. The accessible author manuscript specifies five-fold cross-validation and keeps material from the same song within one partition. It also reports successful participation in MIREX 2005.[^bergstra2006] The official MIREX ranking combines results from different collections and scoring rules, so it cannot be interpreted as a GTZAN accuracy measurement.[^mirex2005] This distinction prevents an apparently precise score from supporting an invalid comparison.

Panagakis, Benetos, and Kotropoulos used spectro-temporal modulation representations, multilinear dimensionality reduction, and an RBF SVM. Under stratified ten-fold cross-validation, non-negative tensor factorization achieved 78.20% on GTZAN, while higher-order singular value decomposition achieved 80.95% on ISMIR2004. These are the best results of different representation variants on different datasets.[^panagakis2008] The study supports considering an RBF SVM for audio classification, but it does not establish that the same performance will be obtained from global MFCC and spectral statistics.

Taken together, these studies motivate a distinction between representation quality and classifier capacity. A more flexible decision boundary may help when the available features contain useful but non-linearly separable information. It cannot restore information that feature extraction has discarded. For example, means and standard deviations preserve aspects of a descriptor's distribution but do not retain the order of its frame-level values. This is a mathematical limitation of global aggregation and motivates examining its practical consequences rather than assuming that additional tuning will resolve every classification error.

The project's representation combines tempo with statistics of zero-crossing rate, RMS energy, spectral centroid, spectral bandwidth, spectral rolloff, MFCC, and chroma. Its advantages are a compact input table and a clearly defined extraction procedure. Its limitations motivate comparison across several classifier families and cautious interpretation of errors involving musically overlapping categories.

## 2.3 Learned Representations and Transfer Learning

Choi and colleagues transferred features from a convolutional network trained on an external music-tagging task. For GTZAN, they reported 89.8% accuracy using transferred convolutional features with an SVM, compared with 66.0% using their MFCC baseline with an SVM. Evaluation used stratified ten-fold cross-validation.[^choi2017] This is especially informative because the comparison changes the representation while retaining the same classifier family. It supports the usefulness of transferred features under the authors' conditions, rather than demonstrating that a convolutional classifier inherently outperforms an SVM.

The external training corpus also changes the comparison with this thesis. A transferred encoder incorporates information learned before the target experiment begins. Its reported score therefore reflects more than the approximately one thousand labelled recordings available locally. This does not diminish the result, but it makes training resources and representation provenance necessary parts of any comparison.

More recent work extends this direction through self-supervised learning. MERT, presented by Li and colleagues at ICLR 2024, combines acoustic and music-related training targets to learn reusable representations. The study evaluates models with 95 million and 330 million parameters across fourteen music-understanding tasks.[^mert2024] It demonstrates the continuing shift toward reusable pretrained encoders, while introducing a substantially different training and computational setting from the current experiment.

These studies suggest a useful future extension: hold the downstream evaluation protocol constant and compare engineered descriptors with embeddings from a pretrained encoder. That would investigate representation quality directly. Within the present scope, a classical pipeline remains useful because it allows a manageable experiment with explicit features, reproducible model fitting, and a clear account of what information enters the classifier. Its contribution should be assessed against those objectives.

## 2.4 Datasets, Benchmark Tasks, and Genre Annotations

GTZAN is convenient for a small experiment because it contains 1,000 excerpts distributed across ten genre categories. Its size supports inexpensive repeated fitting, but its coverage is narrow relative to the diversity of recorded music.[^tzanetakis2002] A result on this collection is consequently evidence about performance on a particular benchmark distribution, rather than a complete account of genre recognition.

Defferrard and colleagues introduced FMA as an open resource containing 106,574 tracks, associated metadata, genre information, and extracted features. Its subsets and supplied partitions support experiments at different scales.[^fma2017] Compared with GTZAN, FMA offers substantially broader material and a more complex genre structure. However, changing the collection also changes class balance, annotation practices, and task difficulty. Scores on the two resources should therefore be reported with their dataset and protocol rather than placed in a single accuracy ranking.

The FMA Challenge further illustrates the importance of specifying the evaluation setting. Its training material comprised 25,000 tracks from FMA medium across sixteen genres. The challenge had two rounds, with the second designed around execution of submitted code on newly collected hidden test material. Log loss was the primary metric, and macro F1 was also reported. The overview contains first-round results and describes the second-round protocol.[^fmachallenge2018] It should not be cited as a complete report of final second-round outcomes or as a single fixed split of the entire FMA collection.

Genre labels themselves are another source of variation. The MediaEval 2017 AcousticBrainz Genre Task used annotations from AllMusic, Discogs, Last.fm, and Tagtraum, allowing systems to be evaluated against different sources of genre information. Although the data included genre and subgenre relationships, the task explicitly did not use hierarchical evaluation measures; it specified precision, recall, and F-scores at the relevant label levels.[^mediaeval2017] Its overview primarily documents the task and baseline setup, with participant findings reported elsewhere.

The implication for this thesis is that the target labels are an operational definition of the classification task. Predicting them successfully does not establish a universal genre taxonomy. Accuracy can assess agreement with the provided labels, while disagreements may reflect both model limitations and ambiguity in the annotation scheme.

## 2.5 Evaluation Reliability and Generalization

### 2.5.1 Dataset Faults and Confounding

Sturm's detailed GTZAN analysis documents repetitions, problematic labels, distortions, and repeated artists.[^sturmgtzan2013] These findings are relevant because random partitioning can distribute closely related material across training and test subsets. A classifier may then exploit similarities associated with a particular recording or performer. The related peer-reviewed journal publication is also included in the bibliography, with its distinct title and publication year; it belongs to the same line of research and is not counted as an independent replication.[^sturm2014]

In a separate evaluation study, Sturm argues that accuracy, precision, recall, and confusion matrices alone cannot establish what a music-recognition system has learned. His analysis examines system behaviour and the relationship between successful label prediction and the intended recognition task.[^sturm2013] This criticism applies even when the reported scores have been calculated correctly. Adding macro F1 broadens the description of performance, but it does not by itself remove confounding.

Rodríguez-Algarra, Sturm, and Dixon subsequently studied interventions involving artist repetition and signal content below 20 Hz. Their results show that confounding effects depend on the representation and can affect conclusions about systems; artist repetition inflated performance, while sensitivity to infrasonic content differed across methods. The authors intentionally avoided hyperparameter optimization to study these effects rather than produce a ranking of optimized classifiers.[^rodriguez2019] This supports examining the data and experimental assumptions alongside the classifier.

For the local feature table, removing exact duplicate rows is a useful but limited safeguard. It does not identify every repeated recording, detect all near-duplicates, or establish artist independence. The table contains no recording identifiers or artist metadata that would enable those checks. The thesis must therefore distinguish duplicate-row removal from a comprehensive audit of the underlying audio collection.

### 2.5.2 Partitioning and Model Selection

The MIREX 2017 audio classification specification provides a concrete example of a stronger partitioning rule: its US Pop and Latin genre tasks use three-fold evaluation with artist filtering. Artists are separated across training and testing within each fold.[^mirex2017] This addresses a different question from a random track-level split. It evaluates transfer to unseen artists within the specified collections, although it still does not establish performance on every other dataset or genre taxonomy.

For the present project, the corresponding methodological principle is to separate model development from final assessment. Standardization and feature selection should be fitted using only the training observations available in each fold. Hyperparameters and model variants should be chosen using development data. A final test set can then assess the selected procedure, provided its results have not also guided that choice.

The implemented scripts satisfy the first distinction through pipelines and training-set cross-validation for hyperparameter search. However, they rank model variants by test accuracy and test macro F1 when identifying the best reported models. This means the test results participate in the reported model comparison and selection. The experiment should therefore be described as a comparison on a common holdout, with a limitation concerning test-based selection, rather than as a strictly independent final assessment of a model chosen entirely through cross-validation. Further model development informed by these results would require a fresh independent evaluation or an appropriately nested design.

### 2.5.3 Augmentation and Domain Differences

Mignot and Peeters examined audio transformations and segmentation for genre classification. In one cross-dataset experiment, training on 1517-Artists and testing on untransformed ISMIR2004 recordings, adding two transformed versions of each training recording improved accuracy from 46.7% to 56.7%. This comparison used harmonized six-class subsets, combined standard descriptors and modulation-spectrum features, and a Gaussian mixture classifier. Benefits depended on the transformation and evaluation setting.[^mignot2019]

Augmentation consequently provides a possible response to limited training data, but not a guarantee of improvement. For any future segmented version of this project, partitions should be created at the source-recording level before related segments or transformed copies are assigned to training and testing. Increasing the number of feature rows does not necessarily increase the number of independent musical examples. The current one-vector-per-recording design avoids creating such segment-level copies, while leaving the separate GTZAN duplication and artist-overlap concerns unresolved.

## 2.6 Comparative Synthesis

Table 2.1 compares representative evidence from the reviewed studies. Numerical values are retained only with the task and conditions needed to interpret them. They are not directly comparable estimates of performance on one shared experiment.

**Table 2.1. Methods, evidence, and limitations relevant to the thesis.**

| Study | Representation and method | Dataset and evidence | Strength and limitation for this thesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tzanetakis and Cook, 2002[^tzanetakis2002] | Timbral, rhythm, and pitch descriptors; statistical classifiers | Ten genres; reported accuracy of 61% | Establishes feature-based feasibility; historical collection and evaluation limit generalization. |
| Bergstra et al., 2006[^bergstra2006] | Aggregated descriptors; AdaBoost | Song-level partitions; five-fold evaluation in the author manuscript | Shows the value of aggregation; its representation and benchmark scores differ from this project's setting. |
| Panagakis et al., 2008[^panagakis2008] | Spectro-temporal tensors, multilinear reduction, RBF SVM | GTZAN: 78.20% with NTF; ISMIR2004: 80.95% with HOSVD; ten-fold CV | Motivates kernel classification; a different representation prevents attributing the results to SVM alone. |
| Choi et al., 2017[^choi2017] | Transferred CNN features or MFCC statistics, followed by SVM | GTZAN: 89.8% versus 66.0%; ten-fold CV | Demonstrates a representation effect; external pretraining changes the resource assumptions. |
| Defferrard et al., 2017[^fma2017] | Open audio collection, metadata, and supplied features | FMA: 106,574 tracks and multiple subsets | Enables broader evaluation; genre structure and distributions differ from GTZAN. |
| Rodríguez-Algarra et al., 2019[^rodriguez2019] | Controlled interventions on artists and infrasonic content | GTZAN; changes in mean recall across experimental conditions | Tests evaluation validity; intentionally untuned systems are not a performance leaderboard. |
| Mignot and Peeters, 2019[^mignot2019] | Augmentation, standard descriptors, modulation features, GMM | One 1517-Artists to ISMIR2004 experiment: 46.7% to 56.7% | Shows a possible cross-dataset benefit; the result is specific to the tested configuration. |
| Li et al., 2024[^mert2024] | MERT self-supervised music representations | Fourteen downstream music tasks | Supplies a recent representation-learning direction; training resources differ substantially from this thesis. |

Three conclusions follow from the comparison. First, representation and classifier must be considered together: an improvement obtained with a different encoder does not isolate the effect of the classifier. Second, dataset cleaning and partitioning influence what a score means. Third, a small, controlled comparison can answer a useful engineering question even when it does not attempt to match systems trained with much greater resources.

## 2.7 Research Gap and Foundation for the Thesis Methodology

The selected literature already contains effective feature-based and learned approaches. It does not justify claiming that classical genre classification is an unexplored problem. The contribution of this thesis is instead a bounded empirical assessment: how well a specified compact representation supports classification in the local dataset, and how the tested classifiers behave when they receive the same observations and evaluation partitions.

The literature provides motivation for this question, but the review does not establish that no previous study has performed a similar comparison. The original work consists of implementing the local extraction and evaluation pipeline, conducting the experiments, and interpreting their outcomes. The descriptors, classifier families, and general validation principles are drawn from established methods.

The research question can therefore be stated as follows:

> To what extent do 61 engineered audio descriptors support ten-class music genre classification, and what performance differences are observed between an RBF SVM and the selected classical baselines under the project's common evaluation protocol?

This question connects to both parts of the operational hypothesis in Section 1.3: reaching at least 70% accuracy and obtaining higher observed test accuracy with the RBF SVM than with the strongest baseline on the common holdout. The 70% threshold is a project-defined engineering criterion, rather than a threshold established by the reviewed publications. Both criteria must be assessed from the project's own results, with the test-based selection limitation described in Section 2.5.2. Meeting them on one split does not establish a population-level guarantee or statistical superiority.

The review motivates the following methodological choices:

1. **Use a shared representation.** Every classifier receives the same 61 predictors, so differences among the local models are assessed without changing the feature extractor. The representation is fixed at one vector per recording.
2. **Compare complementary baselines.** The dummy classifier supplies a reference determined by class frequencies. Logistic regression assesses a linear decision model; a decision tree, random forest, and gradient boosting provide different non-linear alternatives. The RBF SVM is the primary kernel-based model, motivated in part by its use in earlier audio research.[^panagakis2008]
3. **Control preprocessing.** The experiment starts from the unscaled table. Learned transformations are fitted inside training pipelines, including cross-validation folds, rather than using globally standardized exploratory data.
4. **State the unit and limits of cleaning.** The local data contain 1,000 rows; removing 13 exact full-row duplicates leaves 987 observations. This operation does not establish unique artists or unique underlying recordings.
5. **Report the evaluation procedure explicitly.** The configured split is stratified 80/20 with seed 30, producing 789 training and 198 test observations. Five-fold stratified cross-validation is used on the training partition. The test-based ranking limitation described in Section 2.5.2 remains part of the interpretation.
6. **Assess performance beyond one aggregate score.** Accuracy is accompanied by macro precision, macro recall, macro F1, and class-specific errors. These describe different aspects of prediction quality; none substitutes for controlling confounding.[^sturm2013]
7. **Separate observations from explanations.** A confusion pattern can suggest a limitation of global descriptors, but it does not prove the musical cause of an error. Similarly, feature importance describes reliance within the fitted model and should not be interpreted as a causal explanation of genre.

These choices connect the review to the completed engineering work without turning previous publications into guarantees of a particular outcome. A higher observed SVM score would support its usefulness in this experiment. Establishing reliable superiority beyond this split would require additional evidence about variability and independence. Artist-disjoint or cross-dataset testing and comparison with pretrained representations remain justified directions for subsequent work.

## 2.8 Summary

The literature supports engineered audio descriptors as a practical foundation for music genre classification, while showing that representation learning can capture information unavailable to a compact feature table. Dataset scale, annotation conventions, artist overlap, and experimental partitioning are central to interpreting the reported results. Published accuracies consequently provide context rather than directly comparable targets for this thesis.

The project contributes an explicit implementation and comparison of classical models using one fixed audio representation. Its conclusions must remain tied to the local collection, shared holdout, and documented selection procedure. This scope provides a defensible foundation for the subsequent dataset, methodology, and results chapters while identifying concrete extensions: richer representations, independently grouped evaluation, and testing across collections.

Chapter 3 next describes the selected audio collection, the derived feature table, and the available metadata. This establishes the data conditions under which the methodological choices motivated here are applied.
