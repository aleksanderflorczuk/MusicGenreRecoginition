# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Context

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in automated methods for analyzing large digital media collections. In audio-related engineering, this includes the extraction of information from sound recordings, the organization of music libraries, and the development of systems that support search, recommendation, and classification. Music information retrieval is one area of this field, combining digital signal processing with machine learning methods to describe and interpret musical audio.

This thesis focuses on music genre recognition, a task in which a music recording is assigned to one genre category on the basis of measurable properties of the audio signal. From an engineering perspective, the problem can be treated as supervised classification: an audio recording is first transformed into a numerical representation, and a machine learning model then predicts the genre label.

The need for automatic music analysis has increased together with the growth of digital music platforms, streaming services, recommendation systems, and audio archives. Large music collections are difficult to describe manually, especially when new recordings are added continuously. Traditional genre labelling may be performed by experts, publishers, or platform metadata providers, but this process is time-consuming and not always consistent. Genre boundaries are often ambiguous, and the same recording may be interpreted differently depending on musical context, culture, or listening habits. Even with these limitations, genre remains a useful category because it summarizes relationships between timbre, rhythm, instrumentation, production style, and musical convention.

Earlier research established that audio recordings can be represented through engineered descriptors such as timbral features, rhythmic descriptors, and pitch-related information. The GTZAN genre collection became one of the most frequently used benchmark-style datasets for this problem, containing ten common music genres and supporting controlled comparison between classification methods[^1]. However, later studies also showed that evaluation in music genre recognition must be interpreted carefully. High accuracy on one dataset does not automatically prove that a model has learned a general concept of genre, because results may be influenced by duplicate recordings, artist repetition, label ambiguity, or the evaluation protocol[^2].

The present project follows a compact and reproducible engineering approach. Instead of training models directly on raw waveforms or spectrograms, each track is represented by global audio descriptors extracted with the `librosa` library. This decision is practical because raw audio signals are high-dimensional and contain complex temporal and spectral structure. Feature extraction converts them into a fixed-length table that can be used by classical machine learning algorithms. The feature set includes tempo, zero crossing rate, RMS energy, spectral descriptors, MFCC statistics, and chroma statistics.

## 1.2 Motivation and Problem Statement

Music genre recognition is difficult because a genre is not defined by one measurable property. Two recordings may have similar tempo or energy but belong to different genres because of instrumentation, harmony, rhythm, or production style. At the same time, recordings from one genre may differ strongly from each other. This creates a practical question: whether compact audio features are still informative enough to support automatic classification.

The motivation for this work is not only to obtain a high score, but to evaluate a controlled pipeline. Advanced approaches based on spectrograms, transfer learning, or large external datasets may achieve strong results, but they also introduce additional assumptions and resources. A classical feature-based pipeline is appropriate for this thesis because it is feasible on a small dataset, can be reproduced from clearly defined scripts, and remains interpretable at the level of audio descriptors.

The central problem addressed in this thesis is whether ten music genres can be recognized from engineered audio features using classical machine learning methods. The project also investigates whether a non-linear support vector machine with an RBF kernel can improve over simpler baseline models when all models are evaluated under the same data split, preprocessing rules, and validation protocol.

This comparison must be controlled because small methodological differences can change the interpretation of results. Global scaling before the train-test split can leak information from the test data. Duplicate observations can make the final evaluation too optimistic if the same feature vector appears in both training and test subsets. For this reason, preprocessing, duplicate removal, cross-validation, and held-out evaluation are treated as essential parts of the engineering solution.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives

The main aim of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a reproducible machine learning pipeline for music genre recognition based on engineered audio features. The pipeline should classify tracks with acceptable performance while remaining transparent and reproducible.

The specific objectives of the thesis are as follows:

- To extract a fixed-length numerical feature representation from audio recordings belonging to ten music genres.
- To validate and clean the resulting feature table, including checking missing values and removing exact duplicate rows.
- To define a leakage-safe preprocessing strategy based on train-test separation and pipeline-based standardization.
- To train and evaluate baseline classifiers, including a dummy classifier, logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, and gradient boosting.
- To optimize and evaluate an RBF-kernel support vector machine as the primary model.
- To compare the primary model against the strongest baseline using accuracy, macro precision, macro recall, and macro F1.
- To analyze class-level errors, confusion patterns, overfitting indicators, and feature importance.
- To identify the limitations of global audio descriptors and propose directions for future improvement.

The operational hypothesis of the project is that a classifier trained on extracted audio descriptors will achieve at least 70% held-out test accuracy on the selected ten-class music genre dataset, and that the optimized RBF SVM will provide a measurable improvement over the strongest baseline evaluated under identical experimental conditions.

## 1.4 Scope of the Study

The scope of this thesis is limited to supervised classification from a tabular feature representation. The project uses one numerical observation per track. Each observation contains engineered audio descriptors calculated from the corresponding recording. The raw audio files are used only for feature extraction and are not included directly in the model training stage. This scope is feasible because the selected dataset contains 1000 labelled recordings distributed across ten genres.

The study includes the following elements:

- audio feature extraction from genre-labeled `.wav` files;
- construction of a tabular dataset with 61 numerical predictors and one target label;
- exploratory and preprocessing analysis of the feature table;
- duplicate removal and missing-value validation;
- stratified train-test splitting with a fixed random seed;
- cross-validation on the training partition;
- baseline model evaluation;
- RBF SVM optimization;
- final held-out test evaluation;
- analysis of errors, learning behavior, hyperparameter stability, and permutation feature importance.

The study does not include deep learning models, spectrogram-based convolutional networks, transformer-based audio encoders, or real-time deployment. It also does not claim cross-dataset generalization. The conclusions apply only to the selected dataset, the extracted feature representation, and the defined experimental protocol.

Genre is treated as the target label provided by the dataset structure. This is a practical engineering assumption, not a claim that genre categories are objective or universally consistent.

## 1.5 Thesis Structure

Chapter 1 introduces the research context, motivation, objectives, scope, and structure.

Chapter 2 reviews the literature related to music genre recognition, engineered audio descriptors, classical machine learning models, evaluation protocols, and known limitations of benchmark datasets.

Chapter 3 describes the dataset and the types of data relevant to the project, including the audio collection, the derived feature table, metadata assumptions, and reproducibility considerations.

Chapter 4 presents exploratory data analysis, feature engineering, standardization, normalization, duplicate handling, and leakage-prevention rules.

Chapter 5 describes the architecture of the implemented system, including the feature extraction stage, configuration handling, experiment scripts, output artifacts, and reproducibility controls.

Chapter 6 presents the machine learning models used in the project. It defines the classification problem, explains the baseline models, describes the primary RBF SVM model, and discusses the training and evaluation strategy.

Chapter 7 reports and interprets the experimental results. It compares baseline and target models, analyzes class-level errors, discusses feature importance, and identifies limitations of the final system.

Chapter 8 summarizes the thesis, evaluates whether the research aim was achieved, and proposes future work.
